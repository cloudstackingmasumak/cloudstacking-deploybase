Knowing exactly what to do first after a house fire helps La Puente families avoid costly, avoidable mistakes.

The hours right after a house fire are chaotic in a way that is hard to describe until you have lived through it. Adrenaline is still running high, the smell of smoke is everywhere, and it can feel impossible to think clearly about what needs to happen next. Families in La Puente going through this for the first time often do not know where to start.

Having a checklist ready before you need it, or pulling one up in the moment, takes some of the guesswork out of a genuinely overwhelming situation. It gives you a sequence to follow instead of a scattered list of worries competing for attention all at once.

This checklist walks through what actually needs to happen in the first hours, the first days, and the first couple of weeks after a fire touches a La Puente home, organized roughly in the order most families need to handle it.

First hour: safety and immediate needs

Nothing on this list matters until everyone is physically safe. Confirm with fire officials that it is safe to be near or inside the property before doing anything else. If the home is not safe to enter, do not go back in for belongings, no matter how important they feel in the moment.

Once safety is confirmed, take stock of immediate needs. Does anyone need medical attention, even for minor smoke inhalation? Where will the family sleep tonight if the home is not livable? La Puente has hotels along the 60 freeway corridor that can serve as short term housing while longer term arrangements get sorted out, and insurance often covers this if you have additional living expense coverage.

Contact close family or friends who can help in these first hours, whether that means a place to stay, help watching kids or pets, or simply someone to be present while you process what just happened. This is not the moment to handle everything alone.

First few hours: calling the right people

Once immediate safety and shelter needs are addressed, start making calls. Insurance should be near the top of the list, since most carriers have 24 hour claim lines and starting the claim early creates a documented timeline that helps later. Have your policy number ready if you can find it, though the insurer can usually look up your account by address if needed.

Call a restoration company next, ideally one with genuine 24/7 emergency response serving La Puente. The sooner mitigation work starts, water extraction, board up, securing the property, the less secondary damage accumulates on top of what the fire already caused. Ask directly whether they coordinate with your insurance company, since many established crews do this regularly.

If pets or family members need temporary care arrangements beyond the first night, this is also the time to start those calls, since shelters and boarding facilities in the San Gabriel Valley can fill up, especially during high demand periods like wildfire season.

It also helps to notify your employer or your kids' school early, even with a short message, so everyone involved knows why the next few days might look different. La Puente is a tight knit community, and neighbors often step up quickly with meals or spare rooms once they know what happened, but they can only help if someone lets them know.

First 24 hours: documentation

Documentation done early makes the entire insurance process smoother. Photograph every room of the home, not just the areas with visible fire damage, since smoke and water damage often extend well beyond where the flames actually reached. Take wide shots of each room and closer shots of specific damage.

Write down a timeline while memory is still fresh: when the fire started, when the fire department arrived, when you were cleared to re-enter, and any conversations with insurance or restoration companies. This timeline becomes useful later if there is ever a dispute about the claim or the sequence of events.

Start a running list of damaged or destroyed belongings, even a rough one, while details are still clear in your mind. This list does not need to be perfect on day one. It just needs to exist as a starting point that you and your insurance adjuster can refine together over the following days as the fuller scope becomes clear.

First few days: securing the property and belongings

Once emergency mitigation is underway, focus shifts to protecting what is left. Confirm the restoration crew has boarded up any exposed openings and tarped roof damage, since La Puente can see unexpected rain even outside the traditional wet season, and an uncovered opening invites additional damage on top of the fire itself.

Sort through salvageable belongings with guidance from your restoration crew rather than on your own instinct. Some items that look ruined can actually be professionally cleaned and restored, while others that look fine may have hidden smoke or water damage that makes replacement the better option. Do not throw anything away before your insurance adjuster has had a chance to review it or you have clear documentation.

If valuables, important documents, or irreplaceable items survived, consider moving them to a secure location away from the property while repair work continues. Construction activity, even well managed, increases the risk of items being misplaced or damaged further during the rebuild process.

First two weeks: working through the insurance claim

By the end of the first two weeks, most La Puente families are deep into the insurance claim process. An adjuster typically visits the property to assess damage firsthand, and having your documentation ready, photos, the damaged items list, and your own timeline, makes this visit far more productive than starting from scratch in front of the adjuster.

Get a written scope of work from your restoration company that lines up with what the adjuster is assessing. Discrepancies between what a contractor recommends and what an insurer initially approves are common, and having clear documentation on both sides helps resolve these gaps faster rather than letting them stall the whole project.

This is also a reasonable point to start researching contractors if structural rebuilding is needed beyond what your restoration crew handles directly. Getting a couple of quotes, checking licensing, and confirming references matters just as much for rebuild work in La Puente as it did for the initial emergency response.

Ongoing: temporary housing and daily life adjustments

If the home remains unlivable during repairs, settling into temporary housing becomes its own project. Additional living expense coverage, when included in a policy, typically covers a reasonable hotel or rental cost, though there are usually caps and time limits worth understanding early rather than discovering partway through.

Keep routines as normal as possible for kids during this period, even if the family is temporarily relocated somewhere else in or near La Puente. School schedules, regular activities, and familiar faces help provide stability while the home itself is under repair. This part of recovery often gets overlooked amid the practical logistics of insurance and contractors, but it matters for how the whole family experiences the process.

Stay in regular contact with both your insurance adjuster and your restoration or construction crew throughout this stretch. Projects that lose momentum from inconsistent communication tend to drag on longer than necessary, adding stress to an already difficult chapter for a La Puente household.

Final walkthrough before moving back in

Before officially moving back into a repaired home, do a full walkthrough with your restoration or construction crew rather than assuming the work is complete because a calendar date arrived. Check that lingering smoke odor is genuinely gone, not just masked, and confirm any electrical work near the original fire area has been properly inspected and signed off.

Review your updated home inventory against what actually came back into the house versus what was replaced, and keep this record for your own files even after the insurance claim closes. It can be useful for future reference, including if you ever sell the home and need to disclose past damage and repairs.

Take a moment, once the practical checklist is complete, to acknowledge how much work the recovery actually took. A house fire disrupts a family's life in ways that go beyond the physical repairs, and getting a La Puente home back to normal is worth recognizing as the real accomplishment it is.

Keep copies of every document generated along the way, from the initial fire department report to the final invoice from your restoration crew. A complete paper trail protects you down the road, whether that means a future insurance question, a home sale disclosure, or simply peace of mind that the entire process was handled properly from start to finish.
