How Air Scrubbers Work

Air scrubbers are portable or fixed filtration units that actively draw air through filters to remove contamination. The units work by creating negative pressure that pulls contaminated air from the environment into the filtration system. Contaminated air passes through multiple filter stages removing progressively smaller particles. Some air scrubbers include activated carbon filters that absorb odors and volatile organic compounds. The filtered air is released back into the environment, improving overall air quality. Continuous operation of air scrubbers gradually removes all contaminated air and replaces it with filtered air. The effectiveness of air scrubbers depends on run time, unit capacity, and room characteristics. In Medford, professional cleanup teams calculate appropriate air scrubber placement and run time based on property size. Portable air scrubbers are commonly used during cleanup and for days afterward to ensure complete air remediation.

Negative Air Pressure and Containment Strategy

Professional air scrubbing in Medford often includes creating negative air pressure in contaminated areas. The crew seals doorways and vents with plastic sheeting, isolating the affected zone. The air scrubber pulls air from this isolated space, creating negative pressure. This pressure means air flows toward the scrubber rather than spreading to adjacent rooms. Negative pressure prevents cross-contamination and ensures that cleanup activities do not spread biohazards throughout your home. Containment barriers combined with negative air pressure create a controlled cleaning environment in Medford.

HEPA stands for High Efficiency Particulate Air. A true HEPA filter removes at least 99. 97 percent of particles measuring 0. 3 microns or larger. This efficiency rating comes from rigorous laboratory testing and is not marketing language. HEPA filters work through three distinct mechanisms that capture particles of different sizes simultaneously. Large particles are captured through direct interception. As air flows through the dense fiberglass mat of the filter, larger particles follow the air current and collide with filter fibers, becoming trapped. Medium-sized particles are captured through impaction, where particles cannot change direction quickly enough to follow air currents around fibers. The smallest particles are captured through diffusion and electrostatic attraction. Tiny particles collide with air molecules, changing direction randomly until they hit a filter fiber. The fibers also carry a slight electrostatic charge that attracts oppositely charged particles.
